Title: For Me, My Father Was Care

Story
This film is based on my childhood memories of my father and the different ways he cared for me while I was growing up.

Rather than describing his care in general terms, I chose small memories that have stayed with me: going with him to his nearby office, sitting beside him while he worked, waiting for tea and donuts, the gentle way he guided me when I struggled at school, the way he cared for my hands and feet, and how much I missed him when he travelled for fieldwork.

These memories are small moments, but together they represent what my father's care meant to me.

Why This Story Matters to Me
This story is deeply personal because my father was the person who took primary responsibility for caring for me while I was growing up.

Much of what I remember is not one major event, but his consistent presence, patience, attention and gentleness.

Looking back as an adult helped me understand why these memories have remained so strong.

Intended Audience
Primary Audience
My sister and my children.

This is part of our family story, and I want the film to preserve something about my relationship with my father that can remain within our family.

Additional Audience
I also wanted the story to be understandable and emotionally meaningful to people who do not share these memories directly.

For audience testing, I included a friend and a colleague in addition to my sister. This helped me assess whether the story could work for viewers outside the immediate family context.

Intended Emotional Response
I want the audience to first experience the story as a collection of warm childhood memories rather than as an argument about parenting.

The film gradually moves from playful memories, such as accompanying my father to his office and having tea and donuts, toward deeper memories of guidance, physical care, separation and reunion.

By the end, I want the audience to understand why I associate my father so strongly with care, safety and presence.

Broader Idea
In my community, conversations about care for children often emphasize a mother's love and caregiving.

My own childhood experience gives me another perspective. I experienced many of those expressions of tenderness, attention and safety through my father.

The film is not intended to diminish motherhood. It tells my personal experience of fatherhood and care.

Language
The narration is in Amharic because this is the language in which the story feels most personal and natural to me.

English subtitles are included so that people who do not understand Amharic can still follow the story.

Visual Representation
The story is based on real memories, but the AI-generated father and daughter are fictionalized visual representations.

The film does not attempt to reproduce the exact likeness of my father or my childhood appearance.

This decision supports both visual consistency and the ethical handling of real people's likenesses.

Audience Testing
Audience Test 1: Radio Cut
Participant 1
Audience relationship: Sister / primary family audience

Feedback
My sister listened to the Amharic radio cut and enjoyed the story and the overall quality of the generated narration.

Because she does not work in the technology sector, she was especially surprised that the natural-sounding Amharic voice had been generated through an AI-based system.

Her main feedback was that some transitions between memories felt less natural.

She suggested making the narration more conversational by using natural connecting or filler words where appropriate and allowing enough space between one idea and the next.

What I Learned
Her feedback helped me understand that producing clear individual narration clips is not enough.

The movement from one memory to another also affects whether the full story sounds natural and personal.

I learned that pauses, conversational wording, emphasis and transitions are particularly important when using AI-generated Amharic narration.

Human review by a fluent Amharic speaker was necessary to identify issues that were not visible from the written script alone.

Changes Made
Based on her feedback and my own review of the radio cut, I:

reviewed the transitions between shots instead of evaluating each narration clip separately;
introduced an intentional pause after the tea-and-donut memory before moving to the school-guidance memory;
rebalanced the duration of different shots so the new pause could fit without taking the complete film beyond the required duration;
revised selected Amharic phrases to make the narration sound more conversational;
adjusted wording and emphasis in the school-guidance section;
regenerated only the affected narration clips while reusing narration that had not changed; and
retained selected short gaps that could later be supported by instrumental music instead of adding unnecessary narration.
Overall Response
The first audience test confirmed that the story was understandable, personally meaningful and engaging.

It also showed that the transitions and rhythm needed additional refinement before producing the visual scenes and final film.

Audience Test 2: Near-Final Film
Participants
The near-final completed film was shared with two additional viewers:

Participant 2: Friend
Participant 3: Colleague
Together with my sister from the earlier radio-cut test, the film was therefore reviewed by three people.

Feedback
Both participants watched the completed film and gave strongly positive overall feedback.

Both described the film as very good and did not identify a major problem with the story, narration, visuals or overall flow that required another significant revision.

Their responses were particularly useful because they were watching the completed film rather than only listening to the narration.

What I Learned
The two audience tests served different purposes.

The first test with my sister helped identify specific issues in the narration and transitions because she understood the language and the personal context of the memories.

The near-final test helped me evaluate whether the completed combination of narration, visuals, subtitles, motion and music worked for people outside the immediate family context.

The positive response from my friend and colleague gave me confidence that the refinements made after the radio-cut test had improved the coherence of the complete film.

I also learned that audience testing does not always need to result in another change. If viewers understand the story and do not identify a material problem, the appropriate decision can be to stop editing and preserve the version that is working.

Final Decision
Based on the near-final audience feedback, I accepted the current:

story structure;
Amharic narration;
transition timing;
visual sequence;
music direction; and
overall pacing.
I did not make an unnecessary additional revision simply to demonstrate iteration.

The film was therefore locked for final delivery.

Design Decisions From the Radio Cut and Final Film
The radio cut demonstrated that technically valid timing does not necessarily produce natural storytelling rhythm.

For example, the transition from the tea-and-donut memory into the school-guidance memory initially happened too quickly.

The narration for the tea-and-donut scene measured approximately 7.77 seconds, leaving almost no room for the longer pause I originally wanted inside its 8-second allocation.

Rather than speeding up or cutting the natural Amharic voice, I redistributed time across the storyboard and introduced an intentional pause.

This preserved the overall required film duration while improving the transition.

The radio cut also helped identify places where silence should not automatically be removed.

Some gaps later became useful spaces for the instrumental score, particularly around the transition into the deeper caregiving memories.

For the completed film, the music direction was intentionally changed away from a sad cinematic sound.

The final direction uses a slow, calm, Ethiopian-jazz-inspired instrumental style because the story is primarily about warmth, affection, care and gratitude rather than tragedy.

Motion was also designed according to story meaning rather than applying the same effect to every image.

For example:

warm memories use gentle push-ins and pans;
intimate caregiving scenes use minimal movement;
the separation memory uses slower inward movement;
the reunion receives a slightly stronger visual lift; and
the final reflective scene uses a slow pull-back.

These decisions helped the film feel like one continuous memory rather than a sequence of unrelated still images.
